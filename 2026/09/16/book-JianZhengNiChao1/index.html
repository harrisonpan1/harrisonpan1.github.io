Witnessing the Tide - Chapter 1, 2

I believe anyone interested in global macro, especially those trying to build a better investment framework, should take a look at Peng Fu’s new book, Witnessing the Tide: Reflections on the Great Changes in Global Asset Logic.

What caught my attention is that the book does not start with markets. It starts from a much higher level: how ideology, political order, economic structure, and social consensus eventually shape the environment in which financial assets are priced.

I have only worked through the Introduction and the chapters on ideology so far, but they have already changed the way I think about global macro. I want to share some of my takeaways here.

From Market Prices to the Pyramid Framework

Most investors start from the bottom. We look at earnings, inflation, interest rates, central-bank decisions, and market positioning. These are important, but they are only part of a much larger system.

The book organizes that system as a pyramid structure.

  • At the bottom are businesses, industries, trade, markets, and capital.
  • Above them are macro forces such as economic structure, global division of labor, wealth distribution, the financial system, and the monetary system.
  • At the top sit political order, social order, and ideology.

This framework changed the way I think about causality in markets. A company’s margin may fall because of higher input costs. Those costs may come from a supply-chain shift. The supply-chain shift may come from trade policy. And trade policy may reflect deeper changes in domestic politics, national priorities, or attitudes toward globalization. By the time these forces appear in an asset price, many layers of causality have already taken place.

A Framework Matters More Than a View

Another idea I liked in the Introduction is the importance of learning from people who came before us.

The author repeatedly uses the idea of “standing on the shoulders of giants.” The point is not to copy someone else’s conclusions, but to build on accumulated knowledge instead of paying the full cost of discovering everything again.

Markets contain enough randomness that a person can be right for the wrong reasons. Someone can make an enormous return in one year and still have no idea what actually drove the result. Success by itself does not prove that the underlying framework is sound.

This is why the book compares investing to a marathon rather than a sprint. A strong investor is not simply someone who makes a spectacular call. The harder test is whether the person can survive different regimes, understand why a strategy worked, recognize when the underlying conditions have changed, and adjust without losing the discipline of the framework.

Why Ideology Matters for Markets

Before reading this book, I mostly thought about ideology as a political concept. The book pushes the idea much further. It presents ideology as a framework through which people interpret society and the world around them. Individuals with different histories, material conditions, education, cultures, and social positions naturally develop different views. Those views can then become group identities and, eventually, broader forms of social consensus.

Once I started thinking about ideology this way, it became much easier to see why it matters for markets.

Consider some basic questions:

  • How much inequality is acceptable?

  • How much freedom should markets have?

  • When should the government intervene?

  • Should efficiency take priority over equality?

  • How much redistribution is desirable?

  • How important are national security and strategic independence compared with economic efficiency?

These sound like political or philosophical questions. But eventually they turn into tax rates, welfare programs, industrial policy, trade restrictions, capital controls, labor rules, fiscal spending, and monetary policy. And those policies eventually reach asset prices.

That does not mean every market move should be explained through ideology. Most short-term price action does not require that level of analysis. But over longer horizons, changes in political and social thinking can reshape the entire investment environment.

Do Not Turn a View Into an Identity

The part that stayed with me most was the discussion of certainty.

The chapters argue that ideology is shaped by history, social structure, and context. Different systems may work differently under different conditions, and complex economic or political questions cannot always be reduced to a simple right-versus-wrong framework.

I think this applies directly to investing.

Once we become bullish, bullish evidence becomes easier to notice. Once we become bearish, every negative headline starts to look important. A market view can slowly turn into an identity.

That is dangerous.

The question should not be,

How can I prove that I was right?

It should be,

What would make me change my mind?

Markets do not reward consistency of opinion. They reward the ability to adapt when the underlying conditions change.

A good framework therefore needs two qualities at the same time

  • enough structure to guide decisions
  • enough flexibility to be revised.
