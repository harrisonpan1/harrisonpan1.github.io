Witnessing the Tide - Chapter 9, 10, 11

Modern economies have become increasingly dependent on credit expansion. During economic downturns, central banks lower interest rates and inject liquidity into financial markets, while governments expand fiscal deficits to stimulate demand. When financial crises emerge, governments and central banks often become the ultimate backstops, expanding their balance sheets to prevent systemic collapse.

But this raises a fundamental question:

If money can be continuously created and governments can keep borrowing, what ultimately constrains economic growth?

After reading Chapters 9–11 of Witnessing the Reversal, my biggest takeaway is that the modern credit system allows economies to allocate future resources to the present, but credit expansion itself does not necessarily create real wealth.

From Gold Standard to Credit Money

The transition from the gold standard to modern fiat money fundamentally changed how economies operate. Under the gold standard, monetary supply was constrained by the availability of gold. Modern fiat currencies removed this physical limitation, allowing credit creation to support investment, consumption, and economic expansion.

Debt became an essential instrument of growth. Companies could finance innovation, governments could build infrastructure, and households could borrow against future income.

However, credit can create purchasing power today, but it cannot guarantee productivity tomorrow.

When borrowing finances productive investment, credit expansion supports sustainable growth. When it primarily fuels asset speculation or inefficient investment, debt accumulates without generating sufficient future income. This tension also shapes the international monetary system. The collapse of Bretton Woods and the dollar’s transition away from gold convertibility demonstrated how monetary credibility increasingly came to depend on economic strength, financial institutions, and market confidence. The dollar’s reserve currency status gives the United States an extraordinary ability to finance itself through global demand for dollar assets. Yet this privilege does not eliminate the fundamental constraints of debt sustainability.

Fiscal Policy

The evolution of fiscal policy reflects the expanding role of government in modern economies.

Historically, fiscal policy primarily involved collecting taxes to finance basic government functions. Following the Great Depression and the rise of Keynesian economics, governments increasingly used deficit spending to manage economic cycles and support aggregate demand.

This improved economic stability but also introduced new challenges. Fiscal expansion can be productive when it finances infrastructure, education, and technological innovation, creating future economic capacity. However, when government borrowing primarily supports inefficient businesses or persistent structural spending gaps, additional debt may fail to generate sufficient economic returns. Aging populations and growing social welfare obligations further complicate this balance.

The key question is therefore not simply how much governments borrow, but whether additional borrowing creates enough future productive capacity to justify its cost.

The Limits of Monetary and Fiscal Stimulus

Greater liquidity does not necessarily translate into stronger economic growth. Lower interest rates can reduce financing costs, and government spending can stimulate demand. Yet neither guarantees that capital will flow toward productive activities.

Japan’s prolonged stagnation after its asset bubble collapsed illustrates this problem. Despite decades of monetary easing and fiscal intervention, structural challenges continued to constrain economic growth.

When weak demand reflects temporary economic conditions, policy stimulus can be effective. But when the underlying problems involve inefficient resource allocation, demographic pressures, or institutional constraints, additional liquidity alone cannot restore sustainable growth. This is where total factor productivity (TFP) becomes essential. TFP captures improvements in economic efficiency beyond the direct contributions of labor and capital accumulation, reflecting factors such as technological progress, better resource allocation, and organizational improvements.

Monetary and fiscal policies can influence economic activity, but productivity remains the foundation of long-term growth.

The Strongest Bubble: Government as the Ultimate Backstop

One particularly interesting concept is the strongest bubble, the gradual transfer of private-sector risks onto government balance sheets. When households struggle with debt, businesses face widespread failures, or financial institutions approach collapse, governments frequently intervene to stabilize the system.

While necessary during severe crises, repeated interventions can create moral hazard. If investors believe governments will consistently absorb losses, they may become more willing to take excessive risks.

This effectively creates an implicit government put option.Private participants enjoy gains during credit expansions, while some losses during downturns are transferred to the public sector. Over time, this can produce a dangerous cycle: greater risk-taking leads to larger interventions, which further reinforce expectations of future bailouts.

Governments can absorb financial risks, but they cannot eliminate their underlying economic costs. If public debt continues growing without corresponding improvements in productivity and fiscal capacity, sovereign credit itself may eventually come under pressure.

My Takeaway

Credit determines how much future purchasing power an economy can mobilize today, while productivity determines how much real wealth it can ultimately generate. Debt is not inherently harmful. Efficient credit allocation is essential for innovation, investment, and economic development. Problems arise when credit expansion persistently outpaces real wealth creation.

This distinction is particularly important in financial markets. Rising asset prices do not necessarily indicate stronger economic fundamentals. Lower interest rates can increase valuations through declining discount rates, while fiscal stimulus can temporarily support earnings expectations. Neither automatically implies an improvement in productive capacity. If financial markets increasingly rely on continuous liquidity injections and debt expansion rather than productivity improvements, apparent prosperity may simply represent future growth being pulled into the present. Ultimately, understanding economic sustainability requires looking beyond interest rates, fiscal deficits, and liquidity conditions toward the productivity of the investments those policies support.

Money can create purchasing power, and debt can bring future resources into the present. But only sustained productivity growth can generate the real wealth needed to fulfill those promises. That is the fundamental distinction between credit-driven expansion and sustainable economic prosperity.
