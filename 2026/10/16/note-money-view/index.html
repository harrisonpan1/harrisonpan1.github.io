In Memory of Perry Mehrling - From Money View to Global Macro Analysis Framwork

I am deeply saddened by the passing of Perry Mehrling on October 13, an economist I have long admired.

What I appreciate most about his work is how it changed the way I look at financial markets. Traditional macroeconomic analysis tends to focus on growth, inflation, and interest rates. Mehrling’s Money View Theory goes a step further, looking at the credit relationships, balance sheets, and funding mechanisms that keep the financial system running.

His central insight is simple but powerful: the financial system is not just about pricing assets, but also about making payments and meeting obligations. An asset can be fundamentally sound, yet the institution holding it can still run into trouble if it cannot secure funding when needed. This distinction between liquidity and solvency is particularly important in understanding financial crises.

I’ve been trying to incorporate his ideas into my own macro analysis framework, especially when thinking about monetary policy, treasury markets, global dollar liquidity, and financial crises.

In this post, I’d like to share what I’ve learned from Mehrling’s Money View and how I’ve been using it to build a more systematic framework for understanding macro markets.

The Core Idea of Money View Theory

Money Is Credit

At the heart of Mehrling’s Money View is a simple idea: money is credit.

We usually think of money as something we own, whether it’s cash in our wallets or deposits in our bank accounts. But most money in the modern financial system is actually someone else’s liability.

Take a commercial bank making a $1 million loan to one of its customers. Its balance sheet changes as follows

Assets Liabilities
+$1m Corporate Loan +$1m Bank Deposit

The bank creates a loan on the asset side and a deposit on the liability side. From the company’s perspective, that deposit is money it can spend. From the bank’s perspective, it’s a promise to make payments on behalf of its customer.

Now suppose the company transfers that $1 million to someone at another bank. The original bank needs to settle the transaction, typically using reserves held at the central bank. If it doesn’t have enough reserves, it needs to borrow them or obtain funding elsewhere.

This brings us to an important distinction.

Creating credit is not the same as settling payments.

A bank can create deposits through credit, but it still needs access to settlement funds when those deposits move elsewhere. And as we’ll see, the same logic applies to much of the modern financial system.

The Hierarchy of Money

Not all money is created equal. Mehrling argues that money exists in a hierarchy, with different instruments representing claims on different institutions.

A simplified version of the dollar system looks like this:

Level Instruments Role
Central Bank Money Currency, Bank Reserves Final settlement within the dollar system
Commercial Bank Money Bank Deposits Payment promises issued by banks
Money Market Credit Repo, Commercial Paper, MMF Shares Short-term credit with money-like characteristics
Capital Market Credit Corporate Bonds, Loans, MBS Longer-term claims that generally need to be sold or financed to obtain cash

In normal times, we barely notice the hierarchy. Bank deposits can be converted into cash at par. Securities can be sold in liquid markets. Financial institutions can borrow against collateral without much difficulty.
We tend to treat these different instruments as if they were interchangeable.

But during a crisis, those differences become impossible to ignore. Investors start asking which assets can actually be used to make payments, which need to be sold first, and which might become difficult to sell at any reasonable price. Money starts moving toward assets with more reliable liquidity and settlement characteristics.

A financial crisis isn’t necessarily just about falling asset prices. It can also be about the breakdown of the mechanisms that normally allow one form of credit to be converted into another.

The Four Prices of Money

Another interesting part of Mehrling’s framework is that money doesn’t have just one price.

In traditional macro analysis, we spend a lot of time talking about interest rates, the time value of money. Mehrling takes a broader view and identifies four different prices of money.

Price What It Measures Examples
Par The exchange relationship between different forms of money Bank deposits, MMF NAV
Interest/Policy Rate The price of money today relative to money in the future Fed Funds, SOFR, Repo
Exchange Rate The relative price of different currencies USD/JPY, EUR/USD
Price Level Money's purchasing power in terms of goods and services, or domastic inflation CPI, PCE, PPI

These four prices are related, but they don’t always move together.

Think about a situation where the Fed cuts interest rates during a financial crisis. At first glance, lower rates should make financial conditions easier. But what if banks are worried about credit losses and stop lending? What if investors are selling assets to meet margin calls? What if foreign institutions urgently need dollars to repay their liabilities? In that environment, the Fed can cut its policy rate while corporate borrowing costs remain high and the dollar continues to strengthen.

The issue isn’t necessarily that monetary policy has stopped working. It’s that the policy rate is only one of several prices that matter.

This is why I’ve become more careful about statements like “rate cuts are bullish for stocks” or “Fed easing should weaken the dollar”. Those relationships can hold, but we need to understand which part of the financial system the policy is actually affecting.

Liquidity v.s. Solvency

This is probably my favorite idea from Mehrling’s work.

In finance, we usually spend a lot of time asking whether an asset is worth more than what we paid for it, or whether a company has enough assets to cover its liabilities.

These are questions about solvency.

But imagine a fund with $100 million in long-term bonds, $90 million in debt, and positive net worth. On paper, it’s solvent. Now suppose $30 million of its short-term funding matures tomorrow, and lenders refuse to roll it over. Even if those bonds are fundamentally worth $100 million, the fund could still fail if it can’t raise enough cash in time.

That’s the survival constraint, liquidity.

At any given payment date:
Available Cash + Cash Inflows + Available Funding ≥ Cash Obligations

The distinction is straightforward

  • Solvency: Are your assets worth enough to cover your liabilities?
  • Liquidity: Can you actually make the payments you owe today?

In normal markets, solvent institutions can usually obtain financing. But in a crisis, that relationship can break down. A fund may be forced to sell perfectly good assets simply because it needs cash immediately. If enough investors face the same problem, forced selling pushes prices lower, putting pressure on other institutions. What starts as a liquidity problem can eventually become a solvency problem.

This changed how I think about financial risk.

Being right about an asset’s long-term value doesn’t help much if you can’t survive the short term.

Dealers, Repo, and Market Liquidity

The survival constraint becomes even more important when we look at how securities markets actually function.

Suppose a pension fund wants to sell $1 billion of Treasuries. There may not be another investor willing to buy the entire position immediately. At this time, dealers step in, take the securities onto their balance sheets, and gradually sell them to other investors. But dealers need to finance those positions, often through the repo market. They also face capital requirements, risk limits, and balance-sheet constraints.

So market liquidity isn’t unlimited. It depends on the willingness and ability of financial intermediaries to absorb trades.

Repo financing makes this relationship even more interesting.

Suppose a fund owns $100 worth of bonds. With a 2% haircut, it can borrow $98 against them. Now imagine the bond price falls to $95 and the haircut increases to 10%. The same collateral can now support only

95 × (1 − 10%) = $85.5

If the original loan matures and must be refinanced under the new terms, the fund faces a $12.5 funding gap. It needs to provide additional collateral, raise cash, or sell assets.

If many institutions are doing this simultaneously, we get a feedback loop

Collateral Prices ↓ → Funding Capacity ↓ → Margin Calls → Forced Selling → Collateral Prices ↓

Notice that the initial decline in asset prices doesn’t need to be particularly large. Leverage and tighter funding conditions can amplify it.

This is also why Mehrling argued that modern central banks sometimes need to act not just as Lenders of Last Resort, but as Dealers of Last Resort.

Traditional lender-of-last-resort operations help institutions obtain funding. But when an entire securities market loses liquidity, the central bank may also need to support market functioning through repo operations, collateralized lending, or asset purchases.

That doesn’t mean every selloff requires central bank intervention. The difficult part is balancing the financial system’s need for liquidity against the discipline needed to prevent excessive leverage in the first place.

Building a Macro Framework Around the Money View

My Framework

The Money View Theory provides not only a different persective of Monetary Economics, but also a solid Macro analysis framework which I have used a lot.

I find it helpful to think about the economy in three layers.

  • The Real Economy includes consumption, employment, investment, production, inflation, and corporate earnings.
  • The Credit System determines how households, companies, and governments finance their spending. This includes bank loans, corporate bonds, government debt, and leverage more broadly.
  • The Money & Funding System is what allows those credit relationships to function. It includes bank reserves, repo markets, dealers, short-term funding, and the global dollar system.

The three layers constantly interact

Funding → Credit → Spending → Income → Debt Servicing → Funding

When funding is readily available, banks and investors can extend more credit. This supports spending and investment, which can generate stronger income and cash flows. Better cash flows then make borrowers more creditworthy. But the same process can work in reverse. If income deteriorates, borrowers become riskier. Lenders tighten credit conditions. Funding becomes harder to obtain, forcing companies and investors to reduce spending or sell assets.

Based on this, I’ve been developing a six-step process for analyzing macro events.

Step Question What I Look At
1. Identify the Shock What changed? Growth, inflation, monetary policy, credit, funding, fiscal policy
2. Analyze Balance Sheets Who is affected? Assets, liabilities, cash flows, leverage
3. Trace Funding Transmission How does the pressure spread? Repo, collateral, dealers, margin calls, FX swaps
4. Examine the Four Prices of Money Where is the monetary pressure showing up? Par, interest rates, FX, price level
5. Assess Cross-Asset Implications How does this affect markets? Rates, equities, FX, credit, commodities
6. Anticipate Policy Responses What can policymakers do? Rate cuts, QE, repo operations, liquidity facilities

The second and third steps are especially important. These answer the following questions: Who needs cash? Who is providing funding? Who might be forced to sell?

Examples Help to Understand

Take a Bank of Japan rate hike as an example.

The usual analysis starts with the U.S.–Japan interest-rate differential. A narrower differential should, all else equal, make the yen more attractive. But the Money View pushes us to ask more questions.

  • Who has borrowed yen to invest in overseas assets?
  • How much leverage are they using?
  • Are those positions hedged?
  • What happens to their collateral requirements if the yen appreciates sharply?
  • If they need to unwind, which assets do they sell first?

These questions can help explain why a relatively small policy move might trigger much larger changes in global asset prices.

The same applies to U.S. Treasuries.

When the 10-year yield rises, I don’t want to immediately conclude that growth expectations have improved or that investors are worried about inflation.

At least three different forces could be driving it.

Driver Treasury Yields Equities Main Explanation
Growth Shock ↑ Potentially
↑		 Stronger earnings and growth expectations
Inflation / Hawkish Shock ↑ Potentially
↓		 Higher discount rates and tighter expected policy
Funding / Liquidity Shock ↑ Potentially
↓		 Forced selling and constrained intermediation

All three can push Treasury yields higher, but the implications for equities, credit, currencies, and future monetary policy can differ substantially.

So how can we tell them apart?

I like to look beyond price movements and check whether funding and credit markets are confirming the story.

Indicator What I'm Looking For
SOFR / Repo / SOFR-IORB Signs of unusual short-term funding pressure
Treasury Market Depth / Bid-Ask Whether dealers can still absorb trading flows
MOVE Index Changes in Treasury market volatility
IG/HY Credit Spreads Deterioration in credit conditions and risk premia
Cross-Currency Basis Signs of stress in offshore dollar funding
Bank Reserves / TGA / ON RRP Changes in the supply and distribution of dollar liquidity

None of these indicators tells the whole story on its own.

A higher MOVE Index, for example, doesn’t automatically mean there’s a funding crisis. Treasury volatility can rise simply because markets are repricing inflation or monetary policy.

But suppose Treasury yields rise, equities fall, credit spreads widen, repo markets become strained, and Treasury market depth deteriorates at the same time. That would give me more reason to investigate a liquidity-driven selloff rather than a normal repricing of growth expectations.

On the other hand, if yields rise while equities rally, credit spreads tighten, and funding markets remain stable, a growth-driven explanation becomes more plausible.

Historical Examples

Case 1: The 2016 Reflation Trade — Bonds Down, Stocks Up

Following the 2016 U.S. presidential election, investors began pricing in the possibility of tax cuts, fiscal stimulus, deregulation, and stronger economic growth.

Between November 8 and December 15, 2016

Asset Change
U.S. 10Y Treasury Yield 1.88% → 2.60% (+72 bps)
S&P 500 +5.7%

Treasury yields rose sharply, but stocks rallied at the same time.

The explanation is fairly intuitive. Investors expected stronger nominal growth and potentially higher inflation, which pushed Treasury yields higher. At the same time, expectations of stronger corporate earnings and lower taxes supported equities. Although for stocks, higher interest rates increase the discount rate applied to future earnings. But if earnings expectations rise enough, the positive effect can outweigh the higher discount rate.

From the Money View perspective, the key point is that the Treasury selloff wasn’t primarily a funding problem. Investors were repricing the economic outlook and reallocating portfolios. Credit spreads generally tightened, risk appetite improved, and financial intermediaries continued to function normally.

Case 2: March 2020 — When Everyone Wanted Cash

March 2020 is a much more interesting example.

Between March 9 and March 18

Asset Change
U.S. 10Y Treasury Yield 0.54% → 1.18% (+64 bps)
S&P 500 −12.7%

Economic expectations were collapsing. The Fed was cutting rates aggressively. Yet Treasury yields rose sharply while equities continued to fall.

Under normal circumstances, worsening growth expectations should increase demand for Treasuries and push yields lower.

So what happened?

The answer is that investors weren’t just looking for safe assets anymore. They needed cash. The pandemic triggered widespread redemptions, margin calls, and deleveraging. Funds and financial institutions needed to raise liquidity quickly, and Treasuries were among the assets they could sell. But as selling pressure intensified, dealers struggled to absorb the flows. Treasury market depth deteriorated, bid-ask spreads widened, and liquidity became increasingly fragile.

Another source of pressure came from leveraged Treasury cash-futures basis trades. These strategies typically involve buying cash Treasuries, shorting Treasury futures, and financing the cash position through repo. The price differences being exploited are usually small, so leverage is an important part of the strategy. However, when volatility surged, some funds reduced their exposures. Unwinding those positions meant selling cash Treasuries and buying back futures, adding to pressure in the cash market.

The process looked roughly like this

Cash Demand ↑ → Treasury Selling ↑ → Dealer Constraints → Market Liquidity ↓ → Further Deleveraging

The Fed eventually stepped in with large-scale Treasury purchases and other liquidity operations to help restore market functioning.

Case 3: The 2022 UK LDI Crisis — When Hedging Creates a Liquidity Problem

Another example is the UK pension crisis in September 2022.

Following the government’s fiscal announcement, long-term gilt yields rose sharply. According to the Bank of England, 30-year gilt yields increased by roughly 140 basis points over four trading days beginning September 21.

This created serious problems for pension schemes using Liability-Driven Investment (LDI) strategies. LDI is designed to manage the interest-rate sensitivity of pension liabilities. Pension funds often use long-duration bonds, interest-rate swaps, and leveraged structures to hedge their future obligations. In theory, higher interest rates can actually improve a pension scheme’s long-term funding position because the present value of its liabilities falls.

But there was a catch. As yields surged, leveraged LDI funds faced large collateral and margin calls. They needed cash immediately. To raise it, they began selling long-dated gilts. Those sales pushed yields even higher, creating more margin calls and forcing further selling.

Gilt Yields ↑ → Margin Calls ↑ → Forced Selling ↑ → Gilt Yields ↑

Eventually, the Bank of England announced temporary purchases of long-dated gilts to stabilize the market.

Conclusion

What I find most valuable about Mehrling’s Money View is that it helps explain why markets can behave so differently under seemingly similar macroeconomic conditions.

As the three examples show, rising bond yields can reflect stronger growth expectations, a sudden demand for cash, or forced deleveraging. The price movement may look the same, but the underlying mechanisms, and their implications for other assets, can be very different.

This is why I believe macro analysis shouldn’t stop at growth, inflation, and monetary policy. We also need to understand the balance sheets behind market positions, how those positions are funded, and what happens when funding conditions change.

The Money View Theory doesn’t replace traditional macroeconomics or asset pricing. Rather, it helps connect macroeconomic developments to the actual workings of the financial system. By combining these perspectives into the macro analysis, it provides a more systematic framework for understanding how shocks move through credit markets, funding conditions, and eventually into asset prices and the real economy.

There is still a lot for me to learn, but Mehrling’s work has already shaped how I approach these questions. I hope to keep developing this framework as I continue my research. For someone whose ideas have had such an influence on my thinking, I can think of no better way to remember him than to keep learning from his work and putting it into practice.

In Memory of Perry Mehrling.
