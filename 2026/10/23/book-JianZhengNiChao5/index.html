Witnessing the Tide - Chapter 8, 12

The history of the global economy after World War II is often described as a transition from wartime destruction to trade liberalization and globalization. Yet beneath this transformation lies a more fundamental process: the restructuring of global productive capacity, the international division of labor, and the distribution of wealth. The evolution of the U.S. economy, from its postwar Golden Age to the stagflation of the 1970s and the Reagan Grand Cycle of the 1980s, illustrates how an economic system emerges, accumulates structural imbalances, and eventually undergoes institutional and economic adjustments to restore growth.

From Colonialism to Globalization

Competition over resources, markets, and economic gains has long shaped the international economic order. European powers used direct colonial control to secure raw materials, labor, and overseas markets. Industrialization strengthened their technological and military advantages.

The two world wars weakened the European powers economically, and their colonial systems gradually collapsed. The United States emerged as the leading architect of the postwar order, with a large industrial base, accumulated capital, and domestic infrastructure that had largely escaped wartime destruction.

The United States exercised much of its postwar influence through monetary, trade, and financial institutions. The Bretton Woods system placed the dollar at the center of international finance. The Marshall Plan supported European reconstruction, while the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT) laid the groundwork for trade liberalization. These arrangements helped economies recover and supported an international division of labor in which the United States held a central position.

Traditional colonial empires relied on direct control of territory and resources. The postwar system organized more of global production and distribution through trade, finance, and international institutions. A country’s influence depended on its productive capacity and natural resources, as well as its ability to shape rules and capital flows. Its place in the systems that distributed economic gains mattered alongside the wealth it produced.

America’s Golden Age

After World War II, the United States entered a period of strong economic growth that lasted until the early 1970s. Wartime industrial capacity and technologies were redirected toward civilian production. Standardized manufacturing, assembly lines, and the spread of technology raised labor productivity.

Sustaining that expansion required demand to grow alongside productive capacity. Reconstruction in Europe and Japan created overseas demand for American manufacturers. Marshall Plan assistance helped Europe recover and purchase American goods. At home, the baby boom, housing construction, rising automobile ownership, and wider access to education supported consumption growth.

These developments reinforced one another:

  • rising productivity reduced production costs
  • expanding markets encouraged business investment
  • higher employment and wages supported household consumption
  • stronger consumption generated further demand for production

Technical and organizational improvements supported total factor productivity (TFP), while production relations and institutions helped translate productivity gains into wages, consumption, and investment.

Productivity determines how much wealth an economy can create. Turning that capacity into sustained growth also requires sufficient effective demand. Borrowing can support demand and investment, but growth becomes harder to sustain when debt outpaces the gains in productivity and real income needed to service it. A more durable pattern is one in which productivity raises incomes, and those incomes support further consumption and investment.

From Prosperity to Stagflation

As Europe and Japan rebuilt, their manufacturers became stronger competitors to American firms. The spread of technology, industrial upgrading, and better production methods strengthened their positions in automobiles, steel, machinery, and other industries. Economies that had provided markets for American exports increasingly competed with U.S. producers.

Parts of traditional U.S. manufacturing struggled to adjust. Firms tied to older technologies and established markets faced growing pressure, while inflexible labor and production costs made it harder to respond to competition and changing consumer demand. Productivity growth slowed. The oil shocks of the 1970s added to these pressures by raising costs and constraining output. Accommodative monetary policy and entrenched inflation expectations also contributed to persistent inflation. Stagflation combined this inflation with weak growth and high unemployment.

Stagflation exposed the limits of the Keynesian demand-management policies used at the time. Higher government spending or easier monetary policy could stimulate spending, but could not by themselves resolve supply constraints or restore productive efficiency. They could also add to inflation. The oil shocks intensified existing problems, while the difficulties facing manufacturers showed how industrial structures, distribution mechanisms, and institutions could fall behind changes in productive forces. Arrangements that had supported the postwar expansion were becoming harder to sustain.

The Reagan Great Cycle

The policy response began before Reagan took office. In 1979, the Federal Reserve under Paul Volcker shifted toward aggressive monetary tightening to contain inflation. High interest rates contributed to recession and rising unemployment; over time, inflation and inflation expectations declined. From 1981, the Reagan administration pursued tax cuts, deregulation, and other supply-side measures while the Fed’s anti-inflation effort continued. These measures aimed to strengthen business incentives, improve resource allocation, and restore growth potential.

During the 1980s, traditional manufacturers continued to adjust to international competition and the relocation of production. The United States also developed its strengths in high technology, financial services, and capital markets.

George Soros called one set of relationships during this period Reagan Great Cycle, a concept discussed in The Alchemy of Finance. Fiscal deficits supported domestic activity, while the recovery and relatively high interest rates attracted foreign capital and strengthened the dollar. The stronger currency helped restrain import prices but reduced the price competitiveness of American manufacturing. Along with rising domestic demand, it contributed to a wider trade deficit. Capital inflows helped sustain a combination of economic growth, a strong dollar, and fiscal and trade deficits, even as that combination put pressure on domestic producers.

U.S. growth came to rely more heavily on financial markets, the dollar’s international role, and foreign capital to finance domestic spending, as the expansion of domestic manufacturing became less central. A trade deficit means that imports of goods and services exceed exports. The broader current account also includes income and transfers; a deficit on that account is generally matched by net financing from abroad. Such financing can sustain spending beyond what domestic income alone would allow. A deficit therefore does not, by itself, signal an immediate crisis. Its sustainability depends on whether financing remains available, what it costs, and whether it supports future growth.

This pattern carried risks. Persistent external deficits created a continuing need for foreign financing. Fiscal deficits could add to that need when they reduced national saving relative to investment. A strong dollar also put pressure on manufacturers competing with foreign producers. Financial development and international specialization helped the United States adapt to some of its earlier industrial problems, while leaving growth more exposed to debt and changes in capital flows.

My Takeaway

What I take from this history is that a growth model can succeed under one set of conditions and struggle as productivity, technology, and international competition change.

The postwar expansion drew strength from industrial productivity, effective demand, and international institutions that supported them. The 1970s exposed difficulties in adapting that model, alongside failures in inflation control. In the 1980s, monetary stabilization, industrial restructuring, and changes in market incentives accompanied the recovery and the development of new sources of growth. The growing role of debt and international finance brought a different set of risks. Fiscal and monetary policy can manage short-term fluctuations, but lasting growth also requires productivity gains and structural adjustment. Lower financing costs can encourage spending; production relations, institutions, and market incentives help determine whether that money reaches productive activities.

For macroeconomic analysis, I want to look beyond GDP growth, inflation, and interest rates to the mechanisms behind them. How much of an expansion comes from productivity gains, and how much depends on more borrowing? When corporate profits rise, do they reflect better operating efficiency or gains tied to rising asset prices? Are international capital flows financing productive investment or sustaining consumption and debt that will become harder to support?

For investors, monetary policy is one part of the picture. A country’s place in the international division of labor, its industrial competitiveness, and its domestic distribution of income and wealth help explain whether policy changes support lasting growth or delay the adjustment of existing imbalances.

Productivity gains can reshape the international division of labor, changing how economic gains are distributed and creating pressure for institutional adjustment. I would watch for changes in the conditions that sustain prosperity. Growing reliance on debt and financial expansion, without corresponding gains in productive efficiency, may be a warning that the existing growth model is approaching another period of adjustment.
